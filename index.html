Getting around the map

Pan by dragging and zoom with the +/− buttons or a pinch gesture. Two icons sit at the top right of the map, and they control two different things. The layers icon (stacked squares) switches the base map between Satellite, OpenTopoMap, USGS Topo, and Street map, and toggles Trails and Areas on or off: it's for the map itself. The funnel icon next to it controls what's shown on top of that map: Type, Habitat, and Native status, plus Community Sightings and Community Picks. If you're looking to change what the map looks like, use layers; if you're looking to change which pins are showing, use the funnel. When you're zoomed out, nearby pins group into a numbered circle so the map doesn't get cluttered: zoom in, or tap the circle, to spread them back apart. Teal-ringed pins are Community Picks; plain pins are species verified in person for this guide.

Exploring species

Pins are grouped into Trees, Plants & Wildflowers, Animals, Fungi & Lichens, and Landmarks in the menu. Each category is collapsed by default: tap its name to open it, or tap a pin on the map to jump straight to its card and open the right section automatically. Tap a pin or a name in the list to open its field notes, organized into tabs: Overview, Ecology, Uses (edible/medicinal, where relevant), Caution (only shown when there's a genuine hazard), and Connections (links to ecologically related species you can tap through to). Each card also shows a Habitat badge, a Native/Introduced/Invasive status badge, a Role chip summarizing its ecosystem job, and, for Community Picks, a teal badge and a credit box naming the observer.

The funnel button opens a panel with a "Show" section at the top (Community Sightings and Community Picks), followed by Type, Habitat, and Native status checkboxes. They all work together: a pin only shows when it passes every check. The panel also shows how many pins are showing, and Show all turns every filter back on.

Community Picks

Beyond the species verified in person for this guide, the "Community Picks" checkbox in the funnel panel adds real, iNaturalist-documented species into the same category lists, each marked with a teal ring on its map pin and a credit box on its card naming the observer and linking to the original observation. A pin here means the species was documented at that spot, not that it's guaranteed to still be there today. Uncheck the box to hide them and see only the field-verified species.

How It All Connects

This section in the menu lists every real ecological relationship between two species on the map: who eats whom, who pollinates or spreads whom, who depends on whom for food or shelter. Tap either name in a pair to jump straight to its card.

Trail Challenge

A self-guided checklist for your walk. "Starter 10" is a hand-picked set of species that are easy to recognize and represent the park's habitats and native/invasive story; "In season now" shows whatever matches the current month; "All" covers every species and Community Pick in the guide. Tap a box (here or the "I found this one" button on a card) to mark it found: your list is saved only on this device, and nothing is sent anywhere. Reset clears it.

Test Yourself

A short quiz generated fresh from the species cards each time, covering ecosystem roles, fun facts, scientific names, seasonal timing, and the links between species, so it never goes stale as content is added. Each answer comes with a short explanation and a link back to the card. If you've found at least 5 species in the Trail Challenge, you can choose to be quizzed only on those.

Oak Hollow Park by the Numbers

A snapshot of what naturalists have recorded here on iNaturalist: how many observations, how many species, which months see the most activity, and the most-recorded species overall. It's a real sample of what's been documented, not a full census, and it's refreshed periodically from a new export rather than updating live.

Trails & areas

Recorded trails appear as green dashed lines with their distance and elevation gain listed under "Trails" in the menu. Shaded areas mark the boundary of a specific place (like a meadow or a habitat zone) rather than a walking path, listed under "Areas." Both can be toggled on or off from the map's layer switcher.

Community sightings

This optional layer pulls in real, community-submitted observations from iNaturalist near the park - a separate thing from both the verified species and the Community Picks above, since these are raw individual sightings rather than field-guide writeups. Turn it on from the "Show" section in the funnel Filters panel, which then reveals a second set of checkboxes there to show just birds, plants, insects, and so on. Tap any dot to see the photo, date, and a link back to the original observation.

Finding your location

The navigation arrow button near the zoom controls shows your position on the map and follows you as you walk. This needs your permission the first time, only works on the live, secure website (not a locally saved file), and works best on a phone with GPS: accuracy will vary, especially under heavy tree cover.

Saving your own pins

Long-press anywhere on the map to drop a personal pin for something you've found. First, choose whether the pin should sit exactly where you pressed, or at your live GPS location instead (useful if your press was a rough guess but your actual position is more precise). Then pick Plant, Animal, or Not Sure, and add a name or guess and any notes. These show up as solid red circles, the same size as Community Sightings dots but a different color, and are saved privately on this device.

From the "My Pins" section of the menu, you can:

Download for email: exports one pin (or all of them) as a text file to send to the park guide's maintainer for consideration, along with your own photo attached separately.

Export/Upload My Data: a different pair of buttons for your own continuity. Export saves everything to a file you keep, and Upload reads that file back in later, on this device or another one, adding anything new without creating duplicates.

This month at the park

The highlighted box near the top of the menu shows whatever's currently flowering, fruiting, or otherwise in season, based on your device's own clock: tap an entry to jump straight to that species.

Questions, comments, or suggestions

A "Questions, Comments & Suggestions" section near the bottom of the menu has a direct email link for general feedback. If you'd like to suggest a specific plant or animal be added to the guide, use a personal pin's Download option instead, so your coordinates and notes come through with it.

Native vs. Invasive at This Park

Like much of the country, the land around Oak Hollow Park carries the fingerprints of centuries of human movement. Species introduced from other continents, often planted intentionally for ornamental beauty, erosion control, or livestock forage, have spread well beyond where they were planted, and now compete with native species across the region. As species get added to this guide, look for the status badge on each card to see which is which.

That doesn't mean these species are without any value - Mimosa's flowers feed pollinators, and Autumn Olive's and Chinese Privet's berries feed birds - but a landscape dominated by a few aggressive newcomers supports less overall biodiversity than one built on a full community of native species.

Native Introduced Invasive

Look for this status badge on each species card, and a small warning dot on invasive species' map pins. Where relevant, we've noted a native alternative that fills a similar role. You can help track the spread of invasive species through Georgia's Early Detection & Distribution Mapping System (EDDMapS).
